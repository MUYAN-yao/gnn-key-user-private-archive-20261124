# Introduction — SNAM working draft

Selecting key users in a social network requires identifying a small set of seeds that can influence subsequent diffusion. Classical influence maximization targets expected spread [1,8]. In a temporal network, however, seed selection is also constrained by what is observable at the decision time: future interactions are unavailable, and historical connections and activity provide inputs to prediction and selection [19,25,26]. A pipeline's score on a historical graph must therefore be interpreted alongside its prediction task, selection rule, and evaluation conditions.

Learning-based pipelines introduce several potential sources of change, including graph representations, candidate screening, and combinatorial search [6,21,31]. A higher final objective relative to degree ranking or a spread-oriented baseline does not isolate which of these components is responsible. Risk-aware and community-oriented formulations add further decision preferences beyond expected spread [11,12,17,20]. Including these preferences in an objective specifies a selection rule; whether they change the selected users or improve utility under a common reporting objective remains an empirical question.

We address three questions. First, what does future-edge validation establish about learned scores under the recorded temporal and sampling protocol, and does it establish a benefit for seed selection? Second, does candidate reduction preserve full-search selections, and does learned screening add value beyond a simple structural screening rule when the probability graph, reporting objective, and diffusion conditions are controlled? Third, what utility and cost boundaries follow from baseline comparisons, objective-component tests, and stage timings? Our community term counts communities represented by selected seeds. It neither measures communities reached by the resulting diffusion nor imposes a group-fairness constraint.

The evaluated pipeline uses GATv2 [2] to obtain edge-probability estimates and a node prior, then searches a restricted candidate set using a mean-spread objective with a standard-deviation penalty and a seed-community reward. We retain frozen Digg 20k and Higgs 50k comparisons and report separately versioned Higgs studies of timestamp-grouped baseline comparisons, common-world objective ablations, and candidate screening with separate selection and evaluation worlds. A further single post-hoc diagnostic adds same-size weighted out-degree, PageRank, and random candidate pools. The screening arms share learned probabilities; this isolates candidate membership rather than removal of probability learning. Future-edge prediction is evaluated in an external temporal window, but calibration-negative eligibility uses full-period observed edges and a fixed node universe. We therefore interpret this as a retrospective protocol with those information conditions, not a fully future-unaware online pipeline.

The study makes three contributions:

1. An auditable evidence chain separates propagation probability modeling from seed-set selection, linking future-edge validation, baseline comparisons, and matched-objective component tests to recorded artifacts. Passing a prediction gate is not treated as proof that learned probabilities independently improve seed selection.
2. Candidate reduction is evaluated through both selections and stage cost. Across thirty v3 paired runs, a 120-node pool preserves the full-search ordered selections and evaluation scores with lower recorded search-and-final-scoring times. These observations exclude training and preprocessing and do not establish an advantage over simple screening rules.
3. The evidence bounds component attribution. Only some timestamp-grouped Higgs baseline comparisons support an improvement, while the objective ablations and v3 tests do not establish independent component gains. The new single diagnostic records identical GNN and weighted out-degree selections together with complete round-level scores, providing a reproducible mechanism example without population-level inference.

These contributions concern layered validation and conditional empirical findings, not a new GNN architecture, an optimality guarantee, or priority for combining existing components. The evidence uses a two-seed budget and the documented temporal and sampling protocols. Ten stochastic repetitions per split do not constitute ten independent social networks, and overlapping temporal prefixes do not provide independent network samples. The independent contribution of learned probabilities and the full cost of same-size nonlearned screening remain questions for controlled experiments.
