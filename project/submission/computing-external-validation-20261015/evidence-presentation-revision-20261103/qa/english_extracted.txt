Evaluating Candidate-Restricted Influence Maximization:
Effects of Reference Budget and Ranking Precision
Jiayue Sun·Binbin Yong·Junjie An·
Tianyu Bo·Shuai Li
AbstractRestricting seed candidates can reduce influence-maximization cost,
but the resulting quality judgment depends on how both compared seed sets are
computed. We separate the shared selection-world budget from candidate-ranking
precision and assess selected seeds on 10,000 independent worlds. Three external
networks provide the main comparison, with four small contact graphs supplying
separate controls. On ca-GrQc, default PageRank-4k changes from a mean relative
incremental-spread difference of+5.61% to−5.07% as the selection budget in-
creases. A post-hoc tolerance control replaces two candidates and removes this mean
sign reversal, while budget-dependent seed changes persist. Independent evaluation
also distinguishes set instability from utility change: 16 of 40 historical reference
sets change, with a mean spread increase of 0.01111 nodes across all pairs. At the
highest external budget, 20-node degree pools reproduce all 15 reference trajectories
at lower measured selection cost. PageRank losses remain on the directed networks.
These results identify a precision-sensitive quality judgment and quantify when
different seed outputs correspond to different spread, linking candidate compression
to its measured utility and cost.

the spread gained by adding a vertex, making computational cost a central concern.
Lazy evaluation, reuse of sampled realizations and sampling-based optimization ad-

Establishing this trade-off is difficult when the full-candidate reference is itself
selected from finitely sampled diffusion worlds. The observed gap compares two
computed seed sets, and its interpretation depends on how both were obtained. Misat-
tributing that gap can lead to an incorrect judgment about whether a cheaper candidate
pool preserves the reference’s utility.
Selection sampling and candidate ranking enter this comparison at different
stages. In the budget sweeps, increasingWcan change the seeds selected by both the
full-candidate reference and the restricted methods. Their relative spread difference
therefore reflects budget-dependent changes in both selection procedures. Ranking
precision acts before selection, where small score changes can exchange vertices at
the candidate-pool boundary. Seed disagreement alone cannot resolve these effects
because different sets can have similar independently evaluated spread.
We address this evaluation problem by separating seed selection in both arms,
candidate-pool construction and independent assessment of the selected seeds. The
analysis asks whether an apparent budget effect survives a ranking-precision control
and how reference-set changes translate into spread differences. Full and restricted
selection share the propagation graph, sampled-world prefix and tie rule (Fig. 1). In-
dependent evaluation then measures their paired utility difference. This design makes
each comparison explicit while keeping seed eligibility as the only difference be-
tween the two selection arms.
The evidence is drawn from a small, selected sample of real-world networks.
Three external communication, collaboration and voting topologies test the transfer
of earlier findings from four small contact graphs. The contact-graph results provide
separate budget, propagation-parameter and coupling controls. A post-hoc PageRank
analysis tests whether the external budget comparison depends on solver tolerance.

2 Related work
Objectives, sampling and computational efficiency.Kempe et al. establish mono-
tonicity and submodularity of expected spread under the independent-cascade
model, supporting greedy approximation [1]. CELF exploits submodularity to avoid
unnecessary marginal reevaluations [2]. StaticGreedy reuses fixed sampled realiza-
tions throughout selection, preserving a consistent sampled coverage objective [3].
IMM develops a sampling-based solver with approximation guarantees [4]. These
approaches address the computational difficulty of optimization under specified
objectives. Their results motivate careful treatment of the sampled objective, but
do not identify the utility consequence of excluding vertices through a particular
truncated ranking. Our comparison holds the simulator and greedy rule fixed while
changing eligibility, the shared selection budget or ranking tolerance.
Influence estimation and seed-selection strategies.Rui et al. estimate influence from
multiple neighbourhood levels [5], while Fan et al. use discrete Harris hawks opti-
mization for seed selection [6]. Ko et al.’s MONSTOR+ predicts spread on unseen
graphs [7]. These studies offer different ways to estimate influence or search for seed
sets. Their algorithmic objectives differ from assessing a fixed ranking solely as a fil-
ter on seed eligibility. In the latter setting, greedy selection still operates on the com-
plete propagation graph, and candidate coverage becomes part of the comparison. We
therefore distinguish a ranking’s role in defining the pool from the simulator’s role in
evaluating its selected seeds.
4 Jiayue Sun et al.
Evaluation settings and the remaining question.Li et al. survey influence-
maximization methods [8], and Arora et al. examine their quality and computational
cost in a benchmarking study [9]. Yanchenko et al. review temporal-network for-
mulations [10]; Aral and Dhillon study empirical influence models [11]. Together,
these research directions place algorithm comparisons within their diffusion mod-
els, network representations and evaluation settings. Our experiments use observed
topologies with simulated diffusion. They assess sensitivity within that computational
setting and do not estimate realized social influence.
The question addressed here is how a candidate-restriction judgment depends on
two numerical components: finite-world seed selection in both arms and the ranking
that determines restricted eligibility. A comparison at one budget–tolerance setting
does not reveal whether its conclusion persists when either component changes. We
examine this dependence with paired budget and precision controls and assess seed
differences on independent worlds. This extends the evaluation of quality and cost to
the stability of the comparison itself. Online Resource 1 compares this question with
the cited studies’ stated objectives.
3 Methods
This is a simulation-based paired evaluation study. We compare full-candidate and
candidate-restricted greedy selection under shared conditions, then evaluate the re-
sulting seed sets independently. The design separates selection-budget and ranking-
precision sensitivity from candidate coverage, propagation controls and computa-
tional cost. Section 4 specifies the networks, condition matrix and execution settings.
3.1 Paired selection at controlled resolutions
LetG= (V,E)be a directed propagation graph andSa seed set of sizek. Under
the independent-cascade model, each newly activated vertex gets one opportunity to
activate each inactive outgoing neighbour with probabilityp. Propagation stops after
hrounds. For a random worldω, letf h(S;ω)denote total activated vertices, including
seeds. Greedy selection uses the sampled objective
bσW (S) = 1W W∑w=1 fh(S;ω w).(1)
At each round, greedy selection enumerates every unselected eligible vertex and
chooses the largest integer totalW bσW . Ties resolve by lexicographic original node
ID. Full selection usesC=V; restricted selection usesC⊆V. All vertices remain in
the propagation graph. A path identifies one computational repetition and its random-
world families. A condition fixes the graph, path and experimental settings; an arm
specifies a seed-eligibility rule within that condition. Full and restricted arms share
G, the world prefix,kand the tie rule.
In the budget sweeps,Wgoverns seed selection for both the full-candidate ref-
erence and every restricted arm. Each arm selects its seeds anew at eachW, using

Degree ranks distinct outgoing neighbours. PageRank uses the unweighted di-

3.2 World coupling and independent evaluation
Primary selection worlds share draws indexed by world, propagation step, source and
target. This coupling preserves the independent-cascade marginal law for each fixed
seed set, but the realized finite-world objective can be nonmonotone. For example,
initially activating an intermediate vertex can move its outgoing attempt to an earlier
step with a different sampled outcome. Online Resource 1 gives the explicit coun-
terexample. A separate fixed-edge control draws once per edge and world; its finite
objective is coverage by vertices reachable withinhedges and is monotone and sub-
modular. Both implementations use exhaustive enumeration.
Selection-world identifiers form nested prefixes asWincreases. In the exter-
nal study and the 520-condition historical extension, evaluation usesM=10,000
worlds per graph/path/coupling, disjoint from selection and fixed across arms and
budgets. Keys also separate graphs, paths and candidate-pool randomization. Cou-
pling schemes are compared within their own full references.

difference on worldiasd i =f h(SC;η i)−f h(SF;η i). Both sets are selected at the same

3.3 Implementation and cost measurement
The Numba implementation stores candidate IDs, selected sequences, integer round
totals and per-world evaluation values. Reproduction and verification procedures are
provided in Online Resource 1.1
Selection time includes greedy search and lexical candidate sorting, but excludes
graph preparation, world generation and evaluation. We also estimate the cost of a
warm-process run that includes independent evaluation. Separately measured pars-
ing, graph construction and one method’s ranking/pool generation are added to the
recorded costs of selection-world preparation, seed selection, evaluation-world prepa-
ration and evaluation. Setup uses the median of five repeats. These assembled esti-
mates exclude imports, just-in-time compilation, downloads and output writing.
Five randomized repeats at path 1101 andW=320 assess timing variability. Cost
comparisons use this implementation’s exhaustive full-candidate reference. Results
at the tighter PageRank tolerance are used only for quality sensitivity; the timing
comparison uses default tolerance.

Inputs and outputs.Each run takes the processed graphG, its arc directions,k,p,h,

4.3 Randomization, execution and reproducibility
World identifiers separate graphs, paths and selection/evaluation families. Increasing
Wappends selection worlds without resampling the prefix; the selection and eval-
uation identifiers are checked for overlap. Random candidate pools use a separate
randomization key and remain fixed across budgets and coupling controls. Online
Resource 1, “Executable reproduction procedure”, specifies the exact hash keys, iden-
tifier construction and hash-to-draw mapping.
Experiments use one serial worker on Windows with Python 3.13.0, NumPy
2.3.3, NetworkX 3.5, SciPy 1.16.1 and Numba 0.68.0. BLAS/OpenMP threads are
constrained to one. JIT warm-up precedes timed conditions; randomized arm order
reduces systematic order effects. The recorded host has 20 physical CPU cores and
approximately 32 GiB RAM, although the implementation does not use all cores.
Timing and process peak-memory records accompany each condition. Exact wall
times are machine-dependent.

5.1 An apparent budget reversal depends on ranking precision
Figure 2 compares PageRank at default and tighter tolerances at each selection bud-
get, reusing the same-budget full reference for the tolerance comparison. Across bud-
gets, both arms select anew, while the independent evaluation worlds remain fixed.
On ca-GrQc, default PageRank-4k changes from a mean incremental-spread gain of
5.61% atW=80 to losses of 2.53% and 5.07% at 320 and 1280. Tightening tolerance
exchanges two of 20 candidates; the corresponding mean relative incremental-spread

therefore disappears in this numerical control.
Budget still changes selections with the pool formed at the tighter tolerance,

6 Discussion
6.1 A budget comparison can depend on candidate-pool precision
The ca-GrQc reversal disappears after two candidates are exchanged by a tighter
PageRank tolerance. This changes the interpretation of the default budget scan. At
fixedW, the full reference and evaluation worlds are unchanged, so the tolerance
comparison isolates the consequence of the revised restricted pool. AcrossW, both
methods select anew. The remaining budget-dependent seed changes therefore con-
cern selection within each pool, whereas the lost mean sign reversal depends on which
pool is used. The positive strict-tolerance mean atW=1280 also combines positive
and negative path differences; it does not establish a uniform advantage.
StaticGreedy uses shared fixed realizations to maintain a consistent sampled ob-
jective [3]. The present comparison adds an earlier computational step: numerical
ranking determines eligibility before that objective is searched. Fixing the sampled
worlds cannot hold eligibility constant when the truncated ranking changes. The two
controls answer different questions and both matter for interpreting this example. Our
fixed-edge control follows the coverage construction; the step-indexed objective has
the finite-sample limitation described in Methods. These sampled objectives must be
distinguished from the expected-spread guarantee of Kempe et al. [1].
6.2 Seed differences need an independent utility scale
On ca-GrQc, all five reference sets change betweenW=320 and 1280, yet their mean
spread change is 0.12720 nodes. On wiki-V ote, some selection orders change while
the final sets and evaluated spread remain identical. These observations separate two
sources of apparent instability: changing the order of the same seeds and changing
the seeds themselves. Even in the latter case, the signed utility difference requires
measurement. The historical mean of 0.01111 nodes accompanies 16 changed sets
across 40 pairs and includes both positive and negative changes.
Quality–cost benchmarking compares achieved influence with computation [9].
Here, reporting the actual spread alongside agreement distinguishes preservation of
the sampled solution from similarity in utility. The wiki-V ote example makes the dis-
tinction concrete: the degree pool reproduces the reference seeds, whereas the PageR-
ank pools return different seeds and lower spread. Across all five paths, enlarging the
default PageRank pool raises mean total spread from 20.04268 to 47.20590, against
60.73602 for full selection. An agreement count alone would miss the size of that
recovery.

6.3 Coverage and direction explain different parts of the comparison
The default wiki-V ote PageRank-4k pool excludes every full-reference seed atW=
1280; the 5% pool contains only one. Reproducing the full set is therefore impossible
within either pool. Increasing coverage still reduces incremental loss from 73.01%
to 24.28%, showing that membership overlap and recovered utility describe different
outcomes. The degree pool contains and selects the reference seeds with only 20
candidates. Pool size alone does not explain the contrast between the two rankings.
The structural diagnostic is consistent with the direction of the ranking and prop-
agation: PageRank candidates have higher mean in-degree, while degree candidates
have higher mean out-degree. Diffusion follows outgoing arcs in this experiment.
Because direction was not intervened on, this remains an explanation consistent with
the observations rather than an isolated causal effect. The simulated objective also
differs from estimating influence from observed adoption, as in empirical influence
modelling [11].
Selection-time savings have different meanings for these outputs. Degree-4k
saves time while reproducing the tested reference; the PageRank savings accompany
the reported spread losses. Setup and independent evaluation reduce the savings
when included in assembled costs. The relevant comparison therefore depends on
whether a user needs seed selection with prepared worlds or the full evaluated
workflow.
6.4 Scope and unresolved comparisons
The external sample comprises three graphs withk=5,p=.01,h=3 andW≤1280.
Historical contact graphs extend parameter and coupling coverage, but do not estab-
lish the same sensitivity on larger directed networks. At historicalk=20, Kenya’s
4k pool is the complete 75-node graph and invs2013 retains 80/91 vertices. These
high-coverage cases provide limited evidence for aggressive candidate reduction.
The precision control is post-hoc and reuses the original five paths. Paths describe
computational variation on each graph; conditional Monte Carlo errors describe fixed
seed sets. Reference agreement is with a sampled greedy solution, and timings com-
pare this implementation’s exhaustive selection. The saved experiments leave two
specific explanations unresolved: whether the external precision result persists un-
der fixed-edge coupling, and whether changing propagation direction changes the
directed-network ranking contrast. Wider external probability and horizon settings
would also be required to assess transfer of the historical parameter controls.
7 Conclusion
The paired comparisons identify a ranking-precision dependence in a budget-based
quality judgment and quantify the spread consequences of seed changes. On ca-
GrQc, the default mean reversal disappears after the candidate pool changes under
tighter tolerance; budget-dependent seed changes remain. Independent evaluation

distinguishes these changes from their signed utility effects. On the external net-
works, degree pools preserve the highest-budget reference at lower selection cost,
while PageRank illustrates losses and partial recovery through greater coverage. For
the tested simulation settings, reporting candidate membership, both methods’ se-
lection budget, independent spread and scoped costs makes the quality of candidate
compression assessable.
