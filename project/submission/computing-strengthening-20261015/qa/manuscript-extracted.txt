Selection Resolution, Random-World Coupling, and Utility
in Candidate-Restricted Influence Maximization
Jiayue Sun·Binbin Yong·Junjie An·
Tianyu Bo·Shuai Li
AbstractCandidate restriction can accelerate greedy influence maximization, but its
evaluation depends on the finite simulation reference. We examine selection resolu-
tion, random-world coupling, independently evaluated utility and attributable com-
putation cost in a common protocol. An archived experiment on four contact-derived
networks is extended by 520 conditions and 4,680 arm executions, using ten random
paths per real network, selection budgets up to 5,120 worlds and 10,000 disjoint eval-
uation worlds. Controls include alternative candidate rankings, propagation parame-
ters, fixed-edge coupling and two synthetic 2,000-node instances. Between 1,280 and
5,120 selection worlds, 16 of 40 full-greedy final sets changed, while average inde-
pendent spread increased by 0.011 nodes. At the highest budget, weighted-degree 4k
pools had network-specific mean spread differences ranging from -0.610 to -0.005
activated nodes relative to full greedy. Topology-degree and PageRank 4k pools re-
produced 39/40 and 40/40 ordered references, respectively, showing that weighted-
degree losses do not characterize all restrictions. A three-node counterexample shows
that step-indexed shared randomness need not produce a monotone finite-world ob-
jective, motivating an explicit coupling control. The results separate decision repro-
duction from utility and expose the dependence of cost-quality interpretations on
coverage, ranking and timing scope. The supplied tolerance curves are descriptive;
they do not establish practical equivalence, convergence to expected-influence greedy
selection or generality beyond the tested simulated settings.

1 Introduction
Influence maximization selects a small seed set to increase the expected reach of a
stochastic diffusion process [1]. On a fixed graph, practical implementations must
decide both which nodes to consider and how accurately to evaluate them. Candi-
date restriction reduces the search space, while Monte Carlo sampling approximates
spread. These choices interact: a restricted method is often compared with a full-
candidate greedy run whose own decisions depend on a finite simulation sample.
An identical seed sequence can establish reproduction of a named computational
reference, but cannot by itself establish preservation of expected spread. Conversely,
two different sequences can have similar independent utility. This distinction be-
comes consequential when changing the simulation budget changes the full reference,
or when a nominally restricted candidate pool already contains every vertex. A cost
comparison is also incomplete unless it specifies whether ranking, world preparation,
optimization and evaluation are included.
The broader influence-maximization literature already addresses sampling accu-
racy, computational efficiency and benchmarking [2, 8–10, 16]. We therefore do not
present sample sensitivity as a new general discovery. Our question is narrower: how
should candidate restriction be evaluated when the reference, shared random worlds
and practical loss criterion are all implementation choices? We examine member-
ship, independent utility and measured cost together, rather than treating any one as
a surrogate for the others.
An initial four-network experiment motivated this study. We preserve that ex-
periment and distinguish it from a prospectively specified extension with additional
paths, higher budgets, alternative rankings, propagation parameters and a random-
world coupling control. The extension reuses the four observed contact topologies
and adds two explicitly synthetic structural instances. It does not supply new ob-
served information cascades or a random sample of real-world networks.
The contribution is an auditable evaluation of this joint decision problem. First,
we separate seed-set instability from independently measured utility under nested
selection budgets. Second, we examine whether the findings depend on historical
contact weights, candidate coverage or random-world coupling. Third, we provide
loss-tolerance and cost descriptions with path variability and reproducible records.
The implementation is exhaustive per-round greedy enumeration, accelerated without
pruning. No new approximation guarantee, universal candidate threshold or learned
ranking advantage is claimed.

Our reference enumerates every eligible node at every greedy round. It does not
enumerate all size-ksets and is not an exact expected-influence optimum. In addition,
the original simulator shares random draws by edge and propagation step. This cou-
pling can differ from fixing one live-edge graph per world, even when the marginal
diffusion law for a fixed seed set is the intended independent-cascade law. Section 3.2
makes this implementation distinction explicit and supplies a fixed-edge control.
Candidate selection and learning.Candidate sets can be selected using local struc-
ture. Published approaches include the neighbour scale fixed method and discrete
Harris hawks optimization [12, 13]. Learning-based approaches add training, repre-
sentation and tuning choices [10, 14]. We compare simple rankings with matched
candidate counts to isolate the role of candidate eligibility. This does not rank those
published algorithms against each other, and it does not validate a graph neural net-
work selector.
Recent inductive influence estimation, exemplified by MONSTOR+, replaces re-
peated simulation with learned predictions on unseen graphs under IC and LT models
[19]. That approach changes the spread estimator. Our controls retain the simulator
and vary seed eligibility and reference resolution, addressing a complementary eval-
uation question without claiming superiority over a trained estimator.
Diffusion and evaluation.Temporal influence maximization explicitly models evolv-
ing interactions [11]; aggregating a historical contact window does not do so. Like-
wise, observed proximity does not identify an empirical information-influence model
[15]. Our contact records supply topology only. Benchmarking studies have high-
lighted the importance of comparable implementations and evaluation settings [16].
The associated methodological debate further motivates explicit cost and quality def-
initions, but we do not adopt disputed algorithm rankings. Table 1 states the narrower
role of the present study.

3.2 Random-world coupling and its boundary
The original implementation maps a world ID, step and directed endpoint codes to a
reproducible 53-bit uniform variate using fixed 64-bit mixing operations. We call this
step coupling. The same key always supplies the same value, regardless of candidate
pool or execution order. Smaller selection budgets are prefixes of larger budgets. This
construction is a reproducible pseudorandom scheme, not a proof of independence of
all hash outputs.
Step coupling does not imply that each fixed-world objective is monotone. Con-
sider the directed chaina→b→cwith two propagation rounds. Supposea→bis
open at step zero,b→cis closed at step zero and open at step one. Starting from{a}
reaches all three nodes, whereas starting from{a,b}reaches only two:battempts
transmission earlier. Thus, for this world,
{a} ⊂ {a,b},f 2({a};ω) =3>2=f 2({a,b};ω).(2)
The archived hash realizes this example at world ID 5,p=0.5 and endpoint codes
0,1,2. This is a finite-objective counterexample, not evidence that the intended IC
marginal distribution is incorrect. It prevents importing a monotonicity or submodu-
larity guarantee into this sampled objective without further work.
Ourfixed-edge couplingcontrol uses the step-zero variate for each edge through-
out a world. Spread then equals the union of nodes reachable withinhlive-edge hops
from each seed, a coverage objective. With independent edge draws, both couplings
implement the same finite-horizon IC marginal law for a fixedS, but couple differ-
ent seed sets differently. The empirical comparison keeps the coupling label explicit.
Both implementations use full enumeration; no lazy-forward assumption is needed.

3.4 Implementation and timing
The new engine compiles exhaustive simulations on compressed sparse row graphs
with Numba. It preserves the archived hash and tie policy. Integer total spread de-
termines candidate order; at the testedW≤5120, the smallest nonzero mean differ-
ence exceeds 10−6, so this agrees with the archived micro-unit rounding. Audit tests
compare the original Python simulator with the new implementation and compare
fixed-edge outcomes with an independent shortest-path oracle.
World states are materialized once per condition and shared by all arms. We
separately record graph preparation, ranking, shared world preparation, selection
and evaluation. Selection-time savings describe an online scenario with prepared
graph and worlds. The attributed full-pipeline cost charges each arm the common

Arms execute serially in a deterministic randomized order. Five additional ran-

4 Experimental design
4.1 Observed topologies and synthetic controls
The observed topologies derive from the 2013 and 2015 InVS workplace contacts,
SFHH 2009 conference contacts and 2012 Kenyan household contacts [3–6]. Each
undirected event becomes two directed events. The first 65% of complete timestamp
groups are retained, self-loops are removed and repeated endpoints collapse for diffu-
sion. Historical outgoing event counts determine weighted degree; because contacts
are bidirectional, this ranks incident contact frequency. Diffusion uses a uniform edge
probability rather than contact-frequency probabilities.
The resulting graphs have 91, 212, 402 and 75 vertices, respectively. Their di-
rected edge counts are 1,238, 6,938, 11,856 and 1,044. They contain observed con-
tacts, not observed information cascades. The unused later contacts are not a held-out
cascade test set.
Two synthetic graphs have 2,000 nodes each. One uses preferential attachment
with parameter 3 and follows the model of Barabasi and Albert [17]. The other is
a stochastic block model [18]. It has four equal blocks of 500 nodes, with edge
probabilities of 0.012 within a block and 0.0005 across blocks. The random seed
is 20261015 for both. We convert each undirected edge to two directed edges. These
are fixed synthetic instances, not empirical datasets. Their event weights are unity, so
weighted and unweighted degree coincide by construction.

5 Results
5.1 Implementation validation and evidence continuity
All 42 archived output hashes matched the historical validation ledger. The accel-
erated engine reproduced 32 archived arm traces and all 64,000 corresponding per-
world evaluation values exactly. Boundary checks covered empty seed sets, zero and
unit transmission probability, and horizons zero through three. Additional cyclic-
graph checks matched the original simulator, while fixed-edge results matched an
independent shortest-path reachability oracle. These checks support semantic conti-
nuity rather than a new algorithm claim.
The original experiment changed all 14 full-greedy final sets at each adjacent
transition, 20 to 80 and 80 to 320 worlds. Its weighted 2k, 4kand 8kpools matched
0/42, 12/42 and 18/42 ordered traces; after excluding full-pool controls, the respective
counts were 0/42, 6/36 and 6/30. These historical counts are not combined with new
paths as independent replications.
The extension completed all 520 specified conditions and 4,680 arm executions,
retaining 46,800,000 evaluation counts. Source and result hashes, per-round greedy
choices, full-pool consistency and paired statistics passed the independent output val-
idator. Total serial execution lasted 32.7 minutes; the recorded peak process working
set was 780 MiB. The machine had 20 physical CPU cores and approximately 32 GiB
RAM, but experiment computation used one worker. Python 3.13.0, NumPy 2.3.3 and
Numba 0.68.0 were used.

6 Discussion
The practical interpretation of candidate restriction depends on which quantity is to
be preserved. Exact trace reproduction is a statement about a particular finite compu-
tation. Independent spread is a property of its selected set under a specified diffusion
model. Cost is a property of an implementation and a timing scope. A decision to
restrict candidates requires all three to be visible, together with the application’s loss
tolerance.
Reference instability is therefore a diagnostic rather than a sufficient failure cri-
terion. If selected sets change while independent utility changes little, the algorithm
may be moving among similarly useful sets. If utility changes appreciably, a low-
resolution reference can alter the apparent quality of a candidate pool. The path-level
outputs permit these possibilities to be distinguished instead of interpreting any mis-
match as a practically important loss.
The extension narrows the interpretation of the original low-budget findings. Full-
reference utility changes diminished at higher budgets even where membership still
changed. Weighted-degree pools shifted from positive average differences against
the lowest-budget reference to negative differences against the highest-budget refer-
ence. At that highest budget, however, topology-degree and PageRank restrictions al-
most always or always reproduced the full trace. Thus the observed weighted-degree
loss cannot characterize candidate restriction in general. The distinction between
repeated-contact weights and distinct-neighbour topology is consequential under the
uniform-probability simulator.
The coupling audit adds a separate qualification. Consistently reusing random
numbers is not by itself a guarantee that the finite objective has the same structure as
a fixed-live-edge coverage objective. The explicit chain counterexample isolates this
issue without relying on a correlation between ties and changed traces. The fixed-
edge control tests sensitivity to the choice; it does not turn the study into a comparison
against StaticGreedy or invalidate guarantees proved for that method’s own objective.
The ranking result depends on model assumptions. Contact frequency prioritizes
repeated encounters. Uniform-probability diffusion on an aggregated graph instead
depends on distinct neighbours and their connectivity. Comparing the two degree
rankings separates this mismatch from candidate count. It remains a comparison
within the simulator, not evidence that contact frequency is generally uninformative
in real diffusion.
For reuse, we recommend reporting the actual candidate fraction; the named se-
lection budget and coupling; within-path reference transitions and across-path vari-
ability; independent total and incremental spread; and separately attributable costs.
A full-pool match should be identified as a control. Any practical preservation claim
additionally needs a defensible, prospectively fixed loss criterion and a suitable un-
certainty analysis. A large percentage saving is insufficient if the retained incremental
spread is unacceptable.
The remaining limitations are material. Four small contact-derived graphs do not
represent all social networks. Two synthetic 2,000-node instances establish feasibil-
ity at a larger tested size but not web-scale performance or empirical generalization.
Transmission probabilities are illustrative and have not been fitted to observed cas-

cades. The grids do not cover all diffusion regimes or learned selectors. Even 5,120
selection worlds do not certify expected-objective convergence. Ten paths limit dis-
tributional precision, while the primary sign tests address direction rather than mean
superiority. Finally, the code-level acceleration is an implementation tool; the paper’s
contribution remains the evaluation evidence and its interpretation.
7 Conclusion
Evaluating candidate-restricted influence maximization requires a named finite-world
reference and an independent utility assessment. The extended protocol separates
selection resolution, random-world coupling, candidate ranking and actual coverage,
while retaining path-level outcomes and attributable costs. Its findings should be used
to characterize conditional trade-offs, not to infer a universal candidate size or to
equate trace agreement with utility preservation. The supplied records make these
distinctions reproducible within the tested graphs and simulated diffusion settings.
