Selection Resolution Changes Candidate-Truncation
Conclusions in Simulated Influence Maximization
Jiayue Sun·Binbin Yong·Junjie An·
Tianyu Bo·Shuai Li
AbstractCandidate restriction can reduce the cost of greedy influence maximiza-
tion, but its agreement with a simulated full-search reference depends on the simula-
tion budget used to define that reference. We introduce a selection-resolution profile
that jointly reports nested simulation budgets, greedy traces, actual candidate cover-
age, held-out spread, and computation time. On four contact-derived networks under
independent-cascade simulation, every one of 14 full-greedy traces changed when
the selection budget increased from 20 to 80 and again from 80 to 320 worlds. Nom-
inally larger pools matched more traces, although some of those arms covered ev-
ery node; genuinely restricted pools did not match consistently. Restricted searches
saved time but yielded held-out spread differences of both signs. The results dis-
tinguish reproduction of a finite-simulation decision from preservation of diffusion
utility. Candidate-restriction studies should report the named selection budget, stabil-
ity of the full reference, actual candidate coverage, independent utility, and cost. The
findings are limited to the tested simulation setting and do not establish convergence
to exact expected influence.

pected diffusion through a network [1]. In a simulation-based implementation, an-
swering this question involves two computational choices: which nodes to consider

and how accurately to estimate their influence. Restricting the candidate pool reduces
the number of alternatives evaluated at each greedy step. Increasing the number of
simulated diffusion worlds refines the objective estimate. An evaluation of candidate
restriction therefore depends on both the search space and the simulation budget.
The difficulty is that the full-search comparator is itself an estimated decision.
Consider a restricted search that returns the same ordered seed sequence as full greedy
under a small set of simulation worlds. This establishes reproduction of that particular
reference. If full greedy selects a different sequence when the world set is enlarged,
the original agreement does not establish agreement at the new budget. Conversely,
a different sequence need not have worse independently evaluated spread. Decision
reproduction and diffusion utility answer different questions.
We study this distinction for degree-based candidate restriction under the
independent-cascade model. Our research question is:how does the number of
selection worlds affect reference stability, candidate agreement, and the ob-
served cost–utility trade-off?We call this number theselection resolution. Nested
world sets allow us to refine resolution while retaining all earlier worlds. Separate
evaluation worlds measure the spread of the resulting seed sets without reusing the
worlds that selected them.
The methodological context includes the influence-maximization formulation of
Kempe et al. [1] and efficient sampling-based optimization represented by Tang et al.
[2]. The present experiment concerns an enumerative, finite-world greedy implemen-
tation. Its comparator is the stored greedy trace at a specified simulation budget; it
is not an exact expected-influence optimum. This distinction defines the scope of the
evaluation.
The paper organizes reference stability, candidate reproduction, held-out spread,
and cost into a common selection-resolution profile. It applies this profile to a
fixed matrix of four contact-derived networks, three selection budgets, and three
degree-based candidate pools. The resulting evidence identifies a concrete limitation
of reference-based evaluation: every adjacent budget transition changes the ordered
full-greedy trace, while candidate agreement, independent utility, and computation
savings exhibit different patterns.
The contribution is a bounded empirical evaluation, not a new seed-selection al-
gorithm. Specifically, this paper: (i) makes the finite-world full-greedy reference ex-
plicit and tests its stability under nested budget refinement; (ii) separates nominal
candidate-pool size from actual coverage, so full-pool controls are not misreported as
successful truncation; and (iii) combines procedural agreement, conditional held-out
utility uncertainty, and measured timing in a reusable reporting profile. These claims
concern the frozen protocol described below.

Influence-maximization research addresses several distinct sources of computational
difficulty. The classical formulation connects seed selection to the expected reach of
a stochastic diffusion process [1]. The algorithmic survey by Li et al. organizes dif-
fusion models and optimization methods, illustrating why a result for one simulator

and implementation need not describe the entire problem family [9]. Efficient opti-
mization may reduce repeated marginal-gain calculations, change how influence is
sampled, or restrict the eligible seeds. These interventions affect different parts of the
computation and require different evidence of accuracy.
The cost-effective lazy-forward approach developed in outbreak detection ex-
ploits previously computed information to avoid unnecessary objective evaluations
[7]. StaticGreedy emphasizes consistent reuse of sampled diffusion realizations dur-
ing optimization [8]. A fixed collection of worlds defines a reproducible empirical ob-
jective; our nested-world comparison examines whether its greedy solution remains
stable when that collection is enlarged. This distinction positions the study as an eval-
uation of reference sensitivity, building on established simulation-reuse methods.
Sampling-based methods such as IMM pursue efficient influence maximization
with algorithm-specific statistical guarantees [2]. Their sampling procedures and
stopping conditions differ from the small, enumerated world prefixes examined here.
Consequently, the present results cannot be read as a comparison with IMM or as
evidence against its guarantees. Our full-search arm exhausts eligible nodes at each
greedy round, rather than exhaustively enumerating all seed sets or calculating an
exact expected-influence optimum. Naming this comparator precisely is necessary
when interpreting either speed improvements or agreement counts.
Candidate-selection research also includes structural heuristics and population-
based optimization. Rui et al. study a fixed-neighbour-scale approach [12], while Fan
et al. develop discrete Harris hawks optimization for influence maximization [13].
Here, a fixed historical weighted-degree ranking controls the construction of candi-
date pools. Keeping that ranking unchanged allows candidate coverage and selec-
tion resolution to vary without introducing an additional learned representation. The
comparison therefore characterizes the evaluation protocol rather than ranking these
different optimization methods.
Learning-based combinatorial optimization offers another route, reviewed by Li
et al. [10]. A recent example combines lightweight reinforcement learning with prior
knowledge for imbalanced heterogeneous networks [14]. Such methods add choices
about training, representations, and tuning. Extending the present evaluation to
learned selectors would require controlling these choices and matching the historical
information available to simple ranking baselines. The current confirmation matrix
contains degree-based pools only.
Two further distinctions concern realism and benchmarking. Temporal-network
influence maximization explicitly considers evolving interactions [11], whereas our
timestamp split supplies an aggregated historical topology. Empirically motivated
influence models can change seed-selection conclusions [15]; contact observations
alone do not identify such a model. Benchmarking itself has an established literature,
including the study by Arora et al. [16]. We cite that work as evaluation context,
without adopting its broader algorithm rankings. The narrower contribution here is
a fully exposed comparison of reference resolution, actual candidate coverage, seed-
set changes, conditional utility uncertainty, and measured cost on one frozen matrix.
This contribution is descriptive and reproducible, rather than a new approximation
theorem or a claim of general algorithmic superiority.

3.3 A joint interpretation of agreement, utility, and cost
Table 1 summarizes the quantities needed to interpret a candidate restriction. None
substitutes for the others. Exact trace agreement describes a computational decision;
independently evaluated spread describes the resulting seed set; time describes the
expense of obtaining and evaluating that set.
For timing, the measured interval includes greedy enumeration and final inde-
pendent evaluation. Data preparation and degree ranking occur before this interval.
The saving is 1−t C/tV , reported as a percentage. Consequently, these measurements
describe the timed implementation rather than end-to-end preprocessing costs.

sential. Restriction changes which node can be added at a greedy round; it does not
delete ineligible nodes or their incident edges from diffusion. Otherwise, a compari-

4 Experiments
The experiment addresses three linked questions. First, does refinement of a fixed
selection-world path preserve the full-greedy decision? Second, does a degree-
restricted search reproduce the full reference at the same selection budget? Third,
how do its independently evaluated spread and measured computation time compare?
Figure 2 summarizes the crossed comparison structure. Table 2 describes the graph
instances, while Table 3 distinguishes varied factors from shared controls.
4.1 Networks and simulated diffusion
We use four SocioPatterns contact datasets: the 2013 and 2015 workplace deploy-
ments, the 2009 SFHH conference, and 2012 Kenyan households [3–6]. These
records provide observed contacts from which to construct graph instances. They do
not provide observed information cascades.
The first 65% of complete timestamp groups define the historical data. Each undi-
rected contact is represented by two directed edges, and the historical contacts are
aggregated into a graph. Degree ranking uses historical weighted degree. Diffusion
is then simulated on this contact-derived topology using independent cascade with
transmission probabilityp=0.01 and horizon three. The temporal records determine
the historical graph; the reported outcome is simulated spread, rather than a validated
model of temporal information transmission.
Historical weighted degree counts non-self contact events incident to a node in
the bidirectional history; repeated contacts contribute repeatedly to the ranking. Dif-
fusion uses the aggregated endpoint topology with a fixed transmission probability,
rather than treating contact counts as edge probabilities. Spread includes the initially
selected seeds. Each newly activated node has one opportunity to activate an inactive

4.2 Candidate pools and comparison matrix
The restricted pools contain the highest-ranked min(|V|,2k), min(|V|,4k), or
min(|V|,8k)nodes by historical weighted degree. Each pool is constructed before
greedy selection and remains fixed throughout that selection. All arms within a condi-
tion share the graph, simulator, selection worlds, evaluation worlds, and tie-breaking
rule.
The design combines selection budgetsW∈ {20,80,320}with two random-
world paths. All four networks usek=5; the two workplace networks and Kenya
also usek=20. SFHH atk=20 was excluded by a resource preflight before con-
firmation outcomes were examined because preflight projections indicated that in-
cluding it would exceed the four-hour serial ceiling for the confirmation matrix. This
yields seven network–seed-budget settings, 14 nested trajectory blocks, and 42 con-
ditions. Each condition contains full greedy and three restricted arms, giving 168 arm
executions.
Each arm is evaluated on 2,000 worlds disjoint from selection. The study con-
figuration and execution source snapshot were fixed before reading the confirmation
results. Online Resource 1 describes the execution matrix and evidence provenance.
The serial runner executes the full arm followed by the 2k, 4k and 8k pools,
each with a fresh selection cache and a separate evaluation cache. Each arm is timed
once per condition. The reported times therefore describe this execution order and
implementation; repeated timing trials and randomized arm order were not part of
the protocol.
Three comparison units follow from this design. A trajectory block fixes the net-
work, seed budget and path, and contains the three nested selection budgets; there
are 14 such blocks. An arm comparison additionally fixes the selection budget and
contrasts one pool against full greedy; there are 126 such comparisons. A paired eval-
uation difference compares two selected sets on one shared evaluation world. These
units answer different questions and must not be treated as interchangeable replica-
tions.
4.3 Evaluation measures and analysis
We report the fraction of matching ordered traces, tie counts, held-out spread dif-
ferences, and median timing savings. Comparisons across selection budgets use the
same nested trajectory block; comparisons across candidate pools use the same con-
dition.
These summaries describe the tested instances. The four networks are not a ran-
dom sample of a network population, and simulation paths do not increase the number
of independently collected datasets. Pooled condition counts are therefore descrip-
tive, not estimates of a population success probability. The analysis also does not
equate a small point estimate of spread loss with established utility equivalence.

4.4 Does increasing selection resolution preserve the reference?
The full-greedy ordered sequence changed in every tested block at both adjacent bud-
get transitions. No block preserved its sequence from 20 to 80 worlds (0/14), or from
80 to 320 worlds (0/14). Thus, none of these comparisons supplied an unchanged
ordered reference against which candidate restriction could be assessed across reso-
lutions.
Exact best-score ties became less frequent as the selection budget increased: 80
of 160 greedy rounds atW=20 (50.0%), 46 atW=80 (28.8%), and 21 atW=320
(13.1%). The denominator comprises eight traces of five rounds and six traces of 20
rounds. Larger budgets therefore reduced pooled ties without preserving the ordered
traces. The post-hoc analysis also found changed final seed sets in all 14 blocks at
each transition (Section 4.7). Neither result establishes that higher-budget sets are
uniformly better or that ties cause instability.

4.6 Does independent spread follow trace agreement?
Trace disagreement did not have a uniform sign in held-out utility. At 20 selection
worlds, the 2kpool disagreed with every full trace, yet its estimated spread differ-
ence was positive in eight conditions and negative in six. At 320 worlds, it remained
different in every condition, but the split changed to one positive and 13 negative
differences. The same agreement count therefore concealed different utility profiles.
At 320 worlds, mean candidate-minus-full spread differences across the 14 condi-
tions were -0.356, -0.158, and -0.124 activated nodes for 2k, 4k, and 8k, respectively.
For 8k, seven conditions had zero difference and seven had negative differences; none
was positive at this budget. Positive and negative differences occur across the wider
matrix, rather than in every pool–budget combination.
These point estimates show why utility must be inspected separately from trace
agreement. They do not establish practical equivalence or consistent superiority: no
utility-equivalence margin is tested, and the pooled means combine different net-
works and seed budgets. Figure 6 pairs each held-out difference with its measured
time saving, exposing variation concealed by aggregate averages.

5 Discussion and limitations
The results make the evaluation target explicit. An assertion that a candidate pool pre-
serves full greedy should specify whether preservation means the ordered trace, the
final seed set, or diffusion utility. For trace reproduction, the reference also requires a
named selection budget and random-world realization. For utility preservation, inde-
pendent evaluation and an explicit tolerance are needed. Reporting only a matching
trace or a speed reduction leaves these distinct questions unresolved.
The selection-resolution profile offers a practical way to expose this distinction. A
study first refines its full reference on nested worlds, then compares candidate pools
at each common budget, and finally evaluates their selected sets on unused worlds.
Timing completes the comparison. This procedure makes failed reproduction visible
alongside savings and utility estimates. Its role is diagnostic; it supplies neither a new
pruning guarantee nor proof that a particular simulation budget is adequate.
Several limits constrain the evidence. The probability and horizon are fixed, the
pools use only weighted degree, and the graph instances come from four contact
datasets. The experiment does not establish that the same pattern holds for other rank-
ings, diffusion models, parameter regimes, or larger networks. Increasing the world
count to 320 does not establish convergence to expected-influence greedy selection.

16 Jiayue Sun et al.
A systematic characterization of how ties, small score gaps, and graph structure drive
trace changes would require further analysis.
The post-hoc analysis rules out pure reordering as the explanation of reference
changes in this matrix: every adjacent transition also changes final membership. This
does not identify the cause of the change or establish a utility penalty. Likewise, sepa-
rating saturated pools reduces the apparent evidence for successful truncation: many
nominal large-pool matches are full-search controls. Both qualifications strengthen
interpretation while narrowing the supported claim. Similarly, the timing interval ex-
cludes preprocessing and ranking, and the excluded SFHH setting limits the larger-k
comparison. These boundaries are part of the study design and should accompany
reuse of its results.
For reuse, a candidate-pool claim should record the actual candidate fraction
alongside its nominal multiplier. A match at full coverage verifies consistency, while
a match below full coverage establishes reproduction only for the specified empirical
objective. If practical utility preservation is the goal, an acceptable spread-loss mar-
gin should be chosen before confirmatory evaluation. Additional evaluation worlds
can then reduce conditional Monte Carlo uncertainty, whereas additional selection
paths address a different source of variation. Simply increasing one of these budgets
does not substitute for the other.
This separation also guides future experiments. Testing another transmission
probability or diffusion horizon would probe the scope of the observed sensitivity.
Testing larger graphs would probe whether comparable actual candidate fractions
remain useful. A learned selector would require a separate comparison against simple
historical rankings under matched information and tuning budgets. These are distinct
extensions, and the current observations do not predict their outcomes. The frozen
study remains valuable as a transparent diagnostic case, but its modest network
scale and lack of a new algorithmic guarantee should remain explicit in assessing its
contribution.
6 Conclusion
Selection resolution changes the reference used to evaluate candidate-restricted
greedy influence maximization. Across the tested nested budgets, every ordered
full-greedy trace changed, while degree-based restrictions saved computation and
reproduced only a subset of same-budget reference traces. Held-out spread differ-
ences further showed that trace agreement and utility are separate evaluation targets.
Candidate-restriction studies should therefore report the selection budget, reference
stability, independent utility, and computation cost together. This yields a precise,
reproducible account of the observed trade-off within the tested simulation setting.
