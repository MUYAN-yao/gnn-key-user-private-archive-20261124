Evaluating Candidate-Restricted Influence Maximization:
Reference Budgets, Ranking Precision, and External
Topologies
Jiayue Sun · Binbin Yong · Junjie An ·
Tianyu Bo · Shuai Li
Abstract Candidate restriction accelerates greedy influence maximization, yet its
measured quality can depend on choices made before seeds are evaluated. We sepa-
rate two such resolutions: the number of sampled worlds defining the full-candidate
reference and the numerical tolerance used to truncate a ranking. A frozen external
study evaluates degree, PageRank, coverage and random pools on communication,
collaboration and voting topologies with 1,438–7,115 vertices, using nested bud-
gets, disjoint 10,000-world utility evaluation and five paired computational paths. At
W = 1280, 20-node outgoing-degree pools reproduce all 15 full-greedy trajectories,
whereas default PageRank loses 5.07%, 9.01% and 73.01% of incremental spread.
A separately frozen post-hoc control across all three budgets shows why these con-
clusions need resolution labels: on ca-GrQc, default PageRank-4k changes from a
5.61% gain at W = 80 to a 5.07% loss at W = 1280, but strict PageRank remains a
gain at all three budgets (-11.25%, -2.96%, -0.54%). CollegeMsg is unchanged by
the tighter ranking and retains its loss; wiki-Vote retains a 73.96% strict-ranking loss.
Candidate pools are stable at an even tighter tolerance. Thus budget can still change
seed sets and the magnitude of paired utility differences, but the apparent ca-GrQc
direction reversal is not precision-robust. The evidence supports reporting reference
budget, ranking tolerance, coverage, trajectory/set agreement and independent utility
separately; it does not establish a universal safe pool size, convergence or expected-
optimality.

1 Introduction
Greedy influence maximization selects seeds by estimating how much additional dif-
fusion each candidate could generate [1]. Restricting the eligible candidates reduces
that search. Evaluating the restriction, however, requires a computational reference,
and full eligibility with a finite simulation budget is itself an estimated greedy proce-
dure. Its selected sequence can change when the number of sampled worlds changes.
Reproducing that sequence, retaining independent spread, and reducing execution
cost are three different outcomes.
This distinction matters when small-network results are used to justify restric-
tions on larger graphs. A pool of 4k candidates may cover most of a small graph but
less than one percent of a larger one. A ranking can also be numerically close while
exchanging a few candidates at a truncation boundary. Once a greedy procedure op-
erates on the truncated pool, those exchanges can change the seed set. On a directed
graph, standard PageRank emphasizes incoming importance, whereas a seed’s simu-
lated influence follows outgoing arcs. Agreement on contact graphs therefore cannot
resolve topology, coverage, numerical-ranking or reference-resolution effects.
Sampling accuracy and computational efficiency are established topics in influ-
ence maximization [2–5]. We do not claim a new optimization algorithm or a general
discovery that Monte Carlo references can vary. Instead, we ask which empirical
claims survive when candidate eligibility, finite-reference resolution, ranking resolu-
tion, independent utility and cost are evaluated together, and when observations on
small contact graphs are checked on different real topologies.
The study makes three evaluation contributions. First, it separates reference bud-
get and numerical ranking tolerance as distinct resolution choices, then traces their
effects through candidate pools, seed sequences and independently evaluated spread.
Second, it tests the resulting claims across communication, collaboration and voting
topologies while reporting actual coverage, direction and random-world coupling.
Third, it provides an auditable evidence chain that distinguishes trajectory/set agree-
ment, utility preservation and cost. These contributions concern evaluation evidence
and reproducible diagnosis. They do not establish optimality, a learned selector, a
new approximation bound or superiority over efficient influence-maximization algo-
rithms.
2 Related work and positioning
The classical independent-cascade formulation relates expected reach to seed selec-
tion and provides greedy approximation results under suitable objective assumptions
[1]. CELF reduces redundant marginal evaluations using objective structure [6]; Stat-
icGreedy reuses fixed realizations [3]; IMM uses a distinct sampling and guarantee
framework [2]. Our exhaustive per-round reference isolates eligibility effects without
transferring those algorithms’ guarantees to a different finite-world objective.
Candidate selection has been studied using local structure, including the
neighbour-scale approach in Computing [7], and metaheuristic search [8]. In-
ductive spread estimation, such as MONSTOR+, changes the estimator using learned

predictions on unseen graphs [9]. We retain the simulator and vary seed eligibility.
Consequently, the baselines here answer an attribution question, not a ranking of
those published systems. Adding a trained model would require a separate training,
validation and cross-graph testing design.
Benchmarking work illustrates the importance of comparable settings and im-
plementation scope [5]. Temporal-network influence maximization models temporal
structure explicitly [10]; aggregating an event prefix does not do so. Empirical in-
fluence estimation also requires evidence beyond observed proximity or interaction
[11]. All diffusion outcomes in this paper are simulations on observed or synthetic
topologies. Messages, contacts and votes are not treated as observed information cas-
cades. Online Resource 1 gives a source-scoped comparison of the stated focus of
these works; it identifies a combination of evaluation dimensions rather than claim-
ing that every individual dimension is new.
Our diagnostic is narrower than a comprehensive benchmark: it documents how
reference reproduction, independently evaluated reach and attributed costs respond to
budget and ranking resolution on specified graphs. The external validation tests the
scope of the historical observations, including results that contradict an unrestricted
generalization of PageRank’s earlier agreement.

3.2 Random-world coupling and its scope
Primary worlds hash the world identifier, propagation step, source index and tar-
get index. They share draws across arms within a condition. This step-indexed cou-
pling gives each fixed seed set the intended independent-cascade marginal law, but
its realized finite-world objective need not be monotone in the seed set. For a chain
s →u →v with horizon two, take a world in which s →u is open at step zero, u →v is
closed at step zero and open at step one. Starting from {s} reaches all three vertices;
starting from {s,u} activates u too early to reach v and reaches two. This counterex-
ample does not by itself disprove submodularity.
The separately labelled fixed-edge control hashes an edge once per world. Each
resulting finite objective is coverage by nodes reachable within h edges and is mono-
tone and submodular. Both controls use per-round exhaustive enumeration; no CELF
or pruning is used. Fixed-edge results are not silently substituted for the historical
step-coupled experiment.
World IDs derive from a frozen namespace and the graph, path, family and index.
Selection IDs are nested prefixes as W increases. Evaluation uses a disjoint fam-
ily with M = 10,000 worlds per path; its IDs are held across budgets. Shared IDs
do not make different coupling schemes identical worlds. Separate randomization
keys generate candidate pools and arm order. The complete external ID set contains
169,200 distinct identifiers, checked for collisions across graphs, paths and selec-
tion/evaluation families.

3.4 Implementation audit and timing
The adapter imports the previous exhaustive Numba core byte-for-byte. Selection
uses integer totals; earlier micro-unit rounding is consistent with these totals at the
studied budgets. Frozen outputs preserve selected IDs, eligibility pools, every round’s
candidate totals, tie counts, margins and every evaluation-world spread. The inde-
pendent audit reconstructs the graph from gzip text, checks source, graph and result
hashes and candidate identities, regenerates full greedy score matrices and all evalu-
ation arrays, and checks a separate scalar Python oracle for hashing and diffusion on
predetermined evaluation worlds. Matching seed sets must have identical per-world
values.
Two record qualifications are disclosed. The original entry point checked the
frozen data-manifest file but did not enforce its raw-file hashes before running; the
retrospective audit compares actual bytes with both the frozen manifest and every
result’s recorded hashes. The new reproduction wrapper enforces those hashes first.
Also, archived graph preparation includes all rankings and diagnostic metadata.
Adding recorded ranking time again would double count it. Neither frozen code nor
raw results were edited to address these reporting issues.
Selection timings exclude world preparation, graph setup and evaluation. For
a warm-process deployment estimate, we add a separate post-hoc five-repeat mea-
surement of parsing, compressed-sparse-row construction and one method’s rank-
ing/pool construction to the frozen selection-world preparation and selection time. A
verification-inclusive estimate further adds independent-world preparation and evalu-
ation. These assembled costs are hybrid estimates, not directly timed end-to-end runs.
Imports, just-in-time compilation, downloading and output writing are excluded. Five
additional randomized frozen repeats at path 1101, W = 320 check timing variabil-
ity and deterministic outcomes. Reported peak memory is the psutil process-lifetime
peak, not a reset per-condition allocation.

4 Evidence layers and data
4.1 Historical studies
The first archived study has 42 conditions and 168 arm executions, using two paths,
W = 20,80,320 and M = 2000. Its later diagnostics are historical exploratory evi-
dence. A separate locally frozen extension has 520 conditions and 4,680 arm execu-
tions, ten paths on four contact-derived graphs, budgets up to 5120 and M = 10,000.
It includes alternative rankings, propagation parameters, seed budgets, coupling con-
trols and two 2,000-node synthetic graphs. Neither layer is public preregistration.
Contact data originate from the InVS workplace, SFHH conference and rural
Kenya studies [13–16]. Retaining the first 65% of complete timestamp groups yields
91, 212, 402 and 75 nodes, respectively. The synthetic controls use preferential
attachment and a four-block stochastic block model [17, 18]. Their topology-
derived weights are not independent historical-event evidence. These layers remain
immutable and their complete results accompany the local reproduction materials.

4.3 Frozen external matrix
The primary resolution matrix contains 3×5×3 = 45 conditions: three graphs, paths
1101–1105, and W = 80,320,1280 under step coupling. Nine further conditions use
fixed-edge coupling at W = 320 on paths 1101–1103. Every condition evaluates full,
degree-4k, PageRank-4k, degree-5%, PageRank-5% and three random-4k pools. Col-
legeMsg additionally evaluates historical-weighted 4k and 5% pools. This yields 54
conditions and 468 executed arms, all retained. Each uses 10,000 independent evalu-
ation worlds.
The external study does not reach W = 5120. Its purpose is a bounded compari-
son over the frozen budgets, not convergence assessment. Higher-budget convergence
claims have therefore been removed instead of adding an outcome-conditioned exten-
sion. The post-hoc audit and setup timings do not create new confirmatory paths. Data
selection, original source hashes, processed graph hashes, code snapshot, full config-
uration and result indices are retained separately from this submission-preparation
package.
The audit detected changed PageRank candidates when tightening numerical tol-
erance. A separate post-hoc protocol was then frozen before inspecting the resulting
diffusion outcomes. It first reran both PageRank pool sizes at tolerance 10−12, with
at most 1,000 iterations, on all three graphs and five original W = 1280 paths. A sec-
ond protocol, also frozen before its new outputs, added the same restricted arms at
W = 80,320 on the same paths. Thus the two post-hoc layers contain 45 conditions
and 90 restricted-arm executions; they reuse the original graphs, world prefixes, full
references and disjoint evaluation worlds. Pool membership agrees at 10−14. These
diagnostics do not add independent paths, alter graph inclusion or modify the pre-
specified testing family. Original results are preserved.

5.3 Coverage and orientation qualify the PageRank interpretation
At the frozen default tolerance, increasing PageRank eligibility to 5% restores ca-
GrQc’s five ordered references at W = 1280. CollegeMsg’s mean loss falls from 9.01
to 4.28 percent, while wiki-Vote’s falls from 73.01 to 24.28 percent (Fig. 1). Greater
coverage helps within this implementation but does not establish a universal frac-
tion. Historical-weighted CollegeMsg pools have mean difference −0.25224 at 4k,
whereas the 5% pool reproduces the full references. Fixed 4k and fixed coverage
therefore answer different questions.
At W = 1280, tightening PageRank tolerance replaces two of the 20 ca-GrQc
candidates and changes all five selected sets; its mean difference becomes +0.02608
rather than −0.25082. Individual tightened differences range from −0.0408 to
+0.1789, and none of its ordered traces matches full greedy. The positive mean
does not establish superiority or equivalence. Thus the original small loss cannot be
presented as a precision-robust property of PageRank or explained only by pool size.
Complete auxiliary outputs and comparisons appear in Online Resource 1.
Post-hoc structural diagnostics give a concrete direction-related explanation for
wiki-Vote. Its default-tolerance PageRank top 20 have mean in-degree 249.75 and
out-degree 56.2; its degree top 20 have mean in-degree 70.3 and out-degree 489.25.
The former contain none of the five full-reference seeds on any W = 1280 path.

5.5 Coupling and computational cost
The preselected fixed-edge control does not remove the main PageRank loss pat-
tern. At W = 320, its mean differences are −0.16240 for ca-GrQc, −0.93417 for
CollegeMsg and −40.57597 for wiki-Vote. Degree-4k reproduces the fixed-edge ref-
erence on all three CollegeMsg and wiki-Vote paths, but only one of three ca-GrQc
paths. The three-path control is too narrow to conclude that coupling never matters. It
supports reporting the coupling choice explicitly, as motivated by Section 3.2. Online

6 Discussion and limitations
The main contribution is an evaluation distinction: finite-reference budget and numer-
ical ranking tolerance are separate resolution choices. The ca-GrQc control demon-
strates why this distinction changes interpretation. Budget still changes strict-ranking
seed sets and the magnitude of the paired difference, but its default gain-to-loss re-
versal disappears after a stable strict pool is used. CollegeMsg’s unchanged ranking
preserves its loss, while wiki-Vote preserves a large loss under both resolutions. These
outcomes make a more specific recommendation than merely increasing simulation
worlds: report the named budget, ranking tolerance, candidate coverage, sequence/set
agreement and independently evaluated utility together.
External topology changes the conclusion as well. Degree retains exact highest-
budget reference agreement in this setting; PageRank’s earlier contact-graph agree-
ment does not transfer uniformly to the new networks. The persistent wiki-Vote loss
is consistent with incoming-ranking versus outgoing-propagation mismatch and low
full-seed coverage, but the study does not causally isolate direction. The precision-
sensitive collaboration result shows why direction, coverage and numerical ranking
must not be collapsed into one explanation.
Several tempting stronger conclusions remain unsupported. Reference agreement
is not expected-objective optimality. Increasing the budget need not improve every
realized independently evaluated set. The PageRank comparison is against a specified
incoming ranking on fixed directed arcs, not all PageRank-based influence methods.
A small conditional Monte Carlo interval cannot replace selection-path replication.
A nonsignificant five-path sign test cannot prove negligible loss, and an illustrative
tolerance cannot supply a real application’s utility criterion.
The external topology range is substantially larger than the original 75–402-node
contact graphs, but remains three selected networks from one repository. Different
observed relations provide structural diversity, not a probability sample of social sys-
tems. The main external matrix fixes k = 5, p = .01,h = 3 and reaches only 1280
selection worlds. Larger k, other propagation laws, calibrated edge probabilities, di-
rection interventions and further independent graphs may change the conclusions.
Neither 1280 nor the historical 5120 budget certifies convergence to expected-spread
greedy selection.
The code audit reduces the risk of arithmetic, tie-policy, provenance and
coupling-implementation mistakes. It cannot validate an unmeasured real influence
process. The separate scalar oracle samples predetermined worlds while complete
score and evaluation replay uses the frozen core; these checks have different inde-
pendence levels. The ca-GrQc endpoint rule excludes a self-loop-only vertex and is
explicitly limited to the resulting graph. Timing estimates combine measurements
from different runs and exclude cold-start overhead. These are material boundaries,
not reasons to discard adverse results.
For future evaluations, we recommend retaining the actual eligibility fraction,
ranking tolerance, named budget and coupling, per-path reference transitions, in-
dependent total and incremental spread, and a declared cost scenario. If a practical
preservation claim is required, its loss threshold and uncertainty design should be jus-

tified before testing. The present records support that next design without assigning
an unsupported universal threshold.
7 Conclusion
Candidate restriction can reproduce a particular full-greedy computation at much
lower measured selection cost, but its apparent utility depends on how both the refer-
ence and the truncated ranking are resolved. Across three external topologies, strict
ranking removes the ca-GrQc default budget-direction reversal without removing its
budget-dependent seed changes, while CollegeMsg and wiki-Vote retain their respec-
tive losses. Paired evaluation separates changed sequences, changed sets and changed
reach; explicit coverage, coupling and timing scopes prevent those outcomes from be-
ing conflated. This is an auditable characterization of the tested settings, not a new
influence-maximization algorithm, a convergence result or a guarantee of general loss
preservation.
